Solen
Display

A serif drawn for the screen but raised on paper. Solen Display began as a custom face for the Maison Verte identity, then grew into a four-weight family with matching italics drawn by hand from a 1962 specimen of Aldus.

The italic carries the family's argument: that screens can still carry the trace of the hand, and that a typeface made in 2023 has the right to look like it was printed in 1962.

The display weights were drawn first; the text weights are not derived from them — they were drawn separately, at a smaller optical size, with tighter joins and a narrower body. The two cousins share a family name and little else.

"The italic is the argument. The roman is just where the argument lives."

Amassa
Coffee

Twelve farms, one bag system. Amassa asked for packaging that could travel from Minas to Melbourne without losing its origin. The solution: a single die-cut sleeve that wraps a kraft bag, with the farm's coordinates printed in a tear-off tab.

The bag inside stays the same. The sleeve does all the talking — set in Solen Display italic, with the altitude, varietal, and harvest date in a single justified column on the back.

The sleeves are risograph-printed in two colors: a farm-specific ink (chosen by the farmer) plus a universal black. The result is that no two sleeves are exactly alike — a feature the roaster initially called a bug.

"The bag is the same. The sleeve is the farm. The farmer picks the ink."

Festival
d'Avignon

Forty-three shows, forty-three posters, one grid. The 2023 Avignon poster series used a single typographic grid and a single ink color — a riso red — to differentiate forty-three productions across three weeks.

The titles do all the work. Each poster is set in the same display size, the same italic, the same red — but the breaking, the leading, and the kerning are tuned to the rhythm of the show's title. A two-word title sits heavy and centered. A six-word title wraps to four lines and breathes.

The grid is visible only in the negative space. There are no rules. There is only the discipline of having rules.

"One grid. One ink. Forty-three arguments."

Maison
Verte

A cultural center in a former municipal greenhouse. The identity grows: the wordmark is a seed; the signage system is a stem; the publication is the bloom. Across three years, the system has produced more than 200 artifacts without repeating itself.

The mark itself is a three-state form. It opens (for events), closes (for stationary), and turns (for transitions). The construction is geometric — two circles, one curve, one stem — but the behavior is botanical.

The institution speaks in Solen Display italic. The audience speaks in Newsreader. The two never meet on the same line.

"A seed, a stem, a bloom. The mark does what the institution does: it grows."

On
Margin

A 280-page book on the history of marginalia. The margins are wider than the text column. Footnotes appear in the spine. The whole book is an argument for what it describes.

The text column measures 18 × 38 picas. The outer margin measures 28 picas. The marginalia — set in Newsreader italic at 9pt — sits in that outer margin, level with its reference, never below.

The cover is blank. The title appears only on the spine and the title page. The argument is that a book about margins should be unmarked on its own.

"A book about margins should be unmarked on its own."
Fig. 05 — Spread, pp. 84–85
84 · The Medieval Reader
The margin, in the medieval scriptorium, was not empty. It was the room where the reader lived. Glosses, manicules, expunctions, and arguments — all found their place in the marginal zone, level with the text they addressed, never beneath it.

The scribe knew what the printer would forget: that the reader is a co-author, and that the margin is the contract between them.

When the codex gave way to the printed book, the margin shrank. The reader was evicted. The gloss became the footnote, and the footnote was banished to the bottom of the page — a basement apartment in a building that had once been a duplex.
85 · The Footnote Exiled
The footnote, in its modern form, is a compromise. It says: this matters, but not enough. It says: read this, but later. It says: the reader's place is in the basement.

This book proposes a return. The marginalia here sit in the outer margin — level with their reference, never beneath it. The reader is invited back to the room the scribe built.

The argument is in the layout. The text is the evidence.

— M.S., 2024
